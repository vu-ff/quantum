Lecture 5

The particle in a box

What this lecture does

Part I kept every system finite. A particle needs infinitely many dimensions. This lecture builds the answers of Part I for the simplest particle, which moves freely between two walls. Its states are represented by sequences of infinitely many amplitudes. Its Hamiltonian cannot act on every state. It is an operator with a domain, and we prove that it is self-adjoint there.

5.1 Why a particle needs infinitely many dimensions

A particle moving along a line has a position and a momentum. In quantum mechanics, the observables QQQ for the position and PPP for the momentum satisfy the canonical commutation relation QPψ−PQψ=iℏ ψ QP\psi-PQ\psi=\ii\hbar\,\psi QPψ−PQψ=iℏψ(5.1) for every ψ\psiψ in a suitable dense domain. Lecture 7 meets two such operators on the line, with their domain. Here we ask whether the spaces of Part I can hold them. In Cn\C^nCn the domain is the whole space, and (5.1) reads QP−PQ=iℏ11QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\IdQP−PQ=iℏ11.

Proposition 5.1 (No canonical pair of matrices). For every n≥1n\ge1n≥1, there are no complex n×nn\times nn×n matrices QQQ and PPP with QP−PQ=iℏ11QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\IdQP−PQ=iℏ11.

Proof. The trace of a product does not depend on the order of the factors: Tr⁡(QP)=∑j,kQjkPkj=∑k,jPkjQjk=Tr⁡(PQ).\Tr(QP)=\sum_{j,k}Q_{jk}P_{kj}=\sum_{k,j}P_{kj}Q_{jk}=\Tr(PQ).Tr(QP)=j,k∑​Qjk​Pkj​=k,j∑​Pkj​Qjk​=Tr(PQ). So Tr⁡(QP−PQ)=0\Tr(QP-PQ)=0Tr(QP−PQ)=0. The trace of iℏ11\ii\hbar\Idiℏ11 is iℏn\ii\hbar niℏn, which is not 000. 

This is Linear Algebra, Prop. 14.24. A particle therefore needs a space of infinite dimension. The argument does not say which space. That choice is a postulate, made below for a particle confined to an interval.

The simplest such particle moves freely between two impenetrable walls, at x=0x=0x=0 and x=Lx=Lx=L. It is the particle in a box.

5.2 Wavefunctions in a box

A state of the particle assigns an amplitude ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x) to each position xxx in the box. The function ψ\psiψ is the wavefunction of the state. Lecture 6 shows that ∣ψ(x)∣2\abs{\psi(x)}^2∣ψ(x)∣2 is the probability density for finding the particle near xxx. The wavefunctions form a vector space with the inner product ⟨φ,ψ⟩=∫0Lφ(x)‾ ψ(x) dx, \inner\varphi\psi=\int_0^L\overline{\varphi(x)}\,\psi(x)\,\dd x , ⟨φ,ψ⟩=∫0L​φ(x)​ψ(x)dx,(5.2) which is antilinear in the first slot, as in Lecture 1. The space is L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L). Its elements are the functions with ∫0L∣ψ∣2dx<∞\int_0^L\abs{\psi}^2\dd x<\infty∫0L​∣ψ∣2dx<∞, and two functions that differ only on a set of zero length count as the same element (Linear Algebra, Def. 13.19). With the norm ∥ψ∥=⟨ψ,ψ⟩1/2\norm\psi=\inner\psi\psi^{1/2}∥ψ∥=⟨ψ,ψ⟩1/2, the space L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L) is a Hilbert space, a complete inner product space.

So an element of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L) has no value at a single point. Changing ψ\psiψ at x=0x=0x=0 alone changes no integral, and it does not change the element. A condition such as ψ(0)=0\psi(0)=0ψ(0)=0 therefore cannot be imposed on the states. It will return as a condition on the domain of the Hamiltonian.

Postulate 5.2 (States of a particle in a box). The states of a particle in the box are the rays of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L).

5.3 Stationary waves

What is the energy of the particle? For a free particle of mass mmm, Schrödinger’s rule is that the energy acts on a wavefunction by ψ↦−(ℏ2/2m) ψ′′\psi\mapsto-(\hbar^2/2m)\,\psi''ψ↦−(ℏ2/2m)ψ′′. We take the rule as given. Lecture 7 explains it from the momentum on the line. A wavefunction of definite energy EEE, a stationary wave, solves −(ℏ2/2m) u′′=Eu-(\hbar^2/2m)\,u''=Eu−(ℏ2/2m)u′′=Eu. Impenetrable walls are modelled by asking the stationary wave to vanish at both walls. Hall (§3.9) motivates this condition by a very deep well.

5.4 Operators with domains

The energies grow without bound, En=n2E1E_n=n^2E_1En​=n2E1​, and this is where the new difficulty comes from. In the coefficients of (5.4), an energy that multiplies each stationary wave unu_nun​ by EnE_nEn​ multiplies each amplitude cnc_ncn​ by EnE_nEn​. For some states the result is not square-summable, so it is the coefficient sequence of no vector of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L). §5.7 gives such a state, with cn∝1/n2c_n\propto1/n^2cn​∝1/n2.

In Part I every observable was a matrix, and a matrix acts on every vector. In infinite dimension, an operator comes with the set of vectors it acts on.

Definition 5.5 (Operator; densely defined). An operator AAA on a Hilbert space H\HilH is a linear map from a subspace D(A)⊆H\dom A\subseteq\HilD(A)⊆H, its domain, into H\HilH. It is densely defined if every vector of H\HilH is the limit of a sequence in D(A)\dom AD(A). Two operators are equal when they have the same domain and the same action on it.

Definition 5.6 (Adjoint). Let AAA be a densely defined operator on H\HilH. Its adjoint A†\adj AA† has the domain D(A†)={φ∈H: there is χ∈H with ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(A)}, \dom{\adj A}=\bigl\{\varphi\in\Hil:\ \text{there is }\chi\in\Hil\text{ with } \inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner\chi\psi\text{ for every }\psi\in\dom A\bigr\}, D(A†)={φ∈H: there is χ∈H with ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(A)},(5.8) and on it A†φ=χ\adj A\varphi=\chiA†φ=χ.

The vector χ\chiχ is unique, because D(A)\dom AD(A) is dense (Exercise Sheet 5). On Cn\C^nCn with D(A)=Cn\dom A=\C^nD(A)=Cn, the adjoint is the conjugate transpose, as in Part I. In infinite dimension the vector χ\chiχ need not exist for every φ\varphiφ, and D(A†)\dom{\adj A}D(A†) records the φ\varphiφ for which it does.

Definition 5.7 (Symmetric; self-adjoint). A densely defined operator AAA is symmetric if ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨Aφ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner{A\varphi}\psi⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨Aφ,ψ⟩ for all φ,ψ∈D(A)\varphi,\psi\in\dom Aφ,ψ∈D(A). On Cn\C^nCn the two notions coincide, and both are the self-adjoint matrices of Part I.

5.7 A state outside the domain

Is every state in the domain of HHH? No. Take the coefficients cn=90π2n2(n=1,2,3,… ).c_n=\frac{\sqrt{90}}{\pi^2n^2}\qquad(n=1,2,3,\dots).cn​=π2n290​​(n=1,2,3,…). By Euler’s sum ∑n1/n4=π4/90\sum_n1/n^4=\pi^4/90∑n​1/n4=π4/90, the coefficients define a unit vector ψ=∑ncnun\psi=\sum_nc_nu_nψ=∑n​cn​un​. But n4cn2=90/π4n^4c_n^2=90/\pi^4n4cn2​=90/π4 for every nnn, so ∑nn4cn2\sum_nn^4c_n^2∑n​n4cn2​ diverges, and ψ∉D(H)\psi\notin\dom Hψ∈/D(H). The vector HψH\psiHψ is not defined.

The energy readings of ψ\psiψ are nevertheless defined, by the observables postulate. By Euler’s sum ∑n1/n2=π2/6\sum_n1/n^2=\pi^2/6∑n​1/n2=π2/6, the mean energy is ∑nEnProb⁡(En)=90π4 E1∑n1n2=15π2 E1≈1.52 E1,\sum_nE_n\Prob(E_n)=\frac{90}{\pi^4}\,E_1\sum_n\frac1{n^2} =\frac{15}{\pi^2}\,E_1\approx1.52\,E_1 ,n∑​En​Prob(En​)=π490​E1​n∑​n21​=π215​E1​≈1.52E1​, which is finite. The domain is therefore not a set of allowed states. Every unit vector of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L) represents a state, its energy can be measured, and Lecture 6 gives its time evolution.

What does the domain mean, then? For ψ∈D(H)\psi\in\dom Hψ∈D(H), Parseval’s identity gives ∥Hψ∥2=∑nEn2∣cn∣2=∑nEn2Prob⁡(En),\norm{H\psi}^2=\sum_nE_n^2\abs{c_n}^2=\sum_nE_n^2\Prob(E_n),∥Hψ∥2=n∑​En2​∣cn​∣2=n∑​En2​Prob(En​), the mean square of the energy readings. So a state lies in D(H)\dom HD(H) exactly when its energy readings have a finite mean square. For the state above the mean is finite and the mean square is not.

Why insist on self-adjoint, and not only symmetric? Physics texts often call an operator Hermitian when it is symmetric. Integration by parts, as in the step on the rule, proves that Schrödinger’s rule is symmetric on the twice continuously differentiable functions that vanish at the walls. It does not prove self-adjointness, which also requires that the adjoint have no larger domain. In infinite dimension the two notions differ. Schrödinger’s rule on those functions alone is an example. It is symmetric and not self-adjoint (Exercise Sheet 5). Quantum Mechanics II, Lecture 7, shows another operator in this box, the momentum with walls.

Summary

  • The box Hamiltonian is self-adjoint on its domain, its eigenvalues are the EnE_nEn​, and it obeys Schrödinger’s rule (Theorem 5.11). It is the only self-adjoint operator that does (Theorem 5.12).
  • A state outside the domain: the domain holds the states whose energy readings have a finite mean square.
