Lecture 2

A driven qubit: magnetic resonance

What this lecture does

A proton in a strong magnetic field is a qubit with two energies. A second field, two thousand times weaker, can turn its spin over completely, provided that it rotates at the right frequency. This lecture explains how. The weak field changes in time, and so does the Hamiltonian. We solve the Schrödinger equation exactly in a frame that turns with the field, and find Rabi’s formula for the probability that the spin has turned over.

Rabi, Zacharias, Millman and Kusch (1938) sent a beam of molecules through a strong magnetic field and a weak oscillating one of fixed radio frequency. When the strong field made the nuclear spins precess at that frequency, the molecules whose spins had turned over were lost from the detected beam. The known radio frequency and the field at the dip gave the nuclear magnetic moment. In papers published in 1946, Purcell, Torrey and Pound, and independently Bloch, Hansen and Packard, reported the same resonance in bulk matter: paraffin and water. In a water sample at room temperature that has reached thermal equilibrium in the static field, the protons have a small net magnetic moment along the field (Lecture 3). A resonant π/2\pi/2π/2-pulse turns this moment into the xyxyxy-plane. After the drive is switched off, the moment turns there and induces a voltage in a coil around the sample, at the frequency ν0/2π\freq_0/2\piν0​/2π. That voltage is the signal of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and of the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of protons in water.
