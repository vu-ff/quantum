Lecture 2

A driven qubit: magnetic resonance

A proton in a strong magnetic field is a qubit with two energies. A second field, two thousand times weaker, can turn its spin over completely, provided that it rotates at the right frequency. This lecture explains how. The weak field changes in time, and so does the Hamiltonian. We solve the Schrödinger equation exactly in a frame that turns with the field, and find Rabi’s formula for the probability that the spin has turned over. Two short pulses with a long wait between them then give fringes far narrower than the resonance.

Rabi, Zacharias, Millman and Kusch (1938) sent a beam of molecules through a strong magnetic field and a weak oscillating one of fixed radio frequency. When the strong field made the nuclear spins precess at that frequency, the molecules whose spins had turned over were lost from the detected beam. The known radio frequency and the field at the dip gave the nuclear magnetic moment. In papers published in 1946, Purcell, Torrey and Pound, and independently Bloch, Hansen and Packard, reported the same resonance in bulk matter: paraffin and water. In a water sample at room temperature that has reached thermal equilibrium in the static field, the protons have a small net magnetic moment along the field (Lecture 3). A resonant π/2\pi/2π/2-pulse turns this moment into the xyxyxy-plane. After the drive is switched off, the moment turns there and induces a voltage in a coil around the sample, at the frequency ν0/2π\freq_0/2\piν0​/2π. That voltage is the signal of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and of the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of protons in water.

2.6 Ramsey’s two pulses

Rabi’s formula locates the frequency ν0\freq_0ν0​ to within the width 2Ω2\Omega2Ω of the resonance. A sharper resonance needs a weaker drive, and so a longer pulse. In Ramsey’s method, two short pulses turn the spin, and between them the spin precesses with the drive switched off, for a long time TTT. The sharpness then comes from TTT, not from Ω\OmegaΩ.

The oscillator that feeds the coils keeps running, and only its connection to the coils is switched. So during both pulses the field is the one of (2.1), as a function of the same time ttt, and one rotating frame R(t)R(t)R(t) serves throughout. The proof of Lemma 2.1 does not use Ω>0\Omega>0Ω>0. So while the drive is off, the frame Hamiltonian is H~=ℏΔ2σz\tilde H=\tfrac{\hbar\Delta}2\sigma_zH~=2ℏΔ​σz​.

As the drive frequency varies, the probability (2.6) rises and falls in a row of fringes. It is 111 when ΔT\Delta TΔT is a multiple of 2π2\pi2π, and 000 when ΔT\Delta TΔT is an odd multiple of π\piπ. Why does the wait matter, if nothing is driven during it? Throughout the wait r~z=0\tilde r_z=0r~z​=0, and a reading along zzz would give ±1\pm1±1 with probability 12\tfrac1221​, whatever Δ\DeltaΔ is. The detuning shows only in the azimuth of r~\tilde{\blochv}r~, the relative phase of ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ and ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ (Example 1.13). The second pulse turns the angle gained during the wait into a height, and the reading along zzz sees it.

Ramsey proposed the method in 1949 for beams of molecules. The two pulses were two short regions of oscillating field at the two ends of the apparatus, and the wait was the molecules’ flight between them. Molecules of different speeds spend different times TTT in flight, so their fringes have different spacings. The average damps the side fringes, while the central one, at Δ=0\Delta=0Δ=0, keeps its full height for every flight time.

Summary

  • The rotating frame: the circular drive becomes a constant Hamiltonian, so for each initial vector the Schrödinger equation has exactly one solution, and it is known (Lemma 2.1).
  • Rabi’s formula: under a circular drive with any Ω>0\Omega>0Ω>0, a spin prepared up is turned over on resonance at the time π/Ω\pi/\Omegaπ/Ω. Off resonance the probability of the turn stays below 111 (Theorem 2.2).
