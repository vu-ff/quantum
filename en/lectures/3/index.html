Lecture 3

Mixtures and the Bloch ball

What this lecture does

Atoms straight from an oven read +1+1+1 with probability 12\tfrac1221​ along every axis, and no state of Lecture 1 does that. Such a beam is a random mixture of states. This lecture shows that everything a measurement can reveal about a mixture is held in one matrix, the density matrix. For a qubit, the density matrices fill the ball inside the Bloch sphere.

3.1 An unpolarised beam

Send the silver atoms straight from the oven into a Stern–Gerlach magnet, with no magnet before it. Whatever the direction m\mvecm of the magnet, half of the atoms read +1+1+1 and half read −1-1−1. A beam with this property is called unpolarised.

No state of Lecture 1 gives this. By Corollary 1.8, the state with Bloch vector r\blochvr reads +1+1+1 along m=r\mvec=\blochvm=r with certainty. So the atoms from the oven are not all in one state.

A natural guess is that each atom leaves the oven in some state, and that the state varies at random from atom to atom. Suppose, for instance, that the oven prepares ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ or ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩, each with probability 12\tfrac1221​. Along an axis m\mvecm, the atoms prepared ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ read +1+1+1 with probability 12(1+mz)\tfrac12(1+m_z)21​(1+mz​), and those prepared ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ with probability 12(1−mz)\tfrac12(1-m_z)21​(1−mz​). In total, 12⋅12(1+mz)+12⋅12(1−mz)=12\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(1+m_z)+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(1-m_z)=\tfrac1221​⋅21​(1+mz​)+21​⋅21​(1−mz​)=21​ for every m\mvecm, as observed. But an oven that prepares ∣+x⟩\ket{+x}∣+x⟩ or ∣−x⟩\ket{-x}∣−x⟩, each with probability 12\tfrac1221​, gives 12\tfrac1221​ for every m\mvecm just as well. Which is it? This lecture shows that no measurement can tell.

In an oven at several hundred degrees, silver atoms collide with the walls and with each other at random, and nothing in the oven picks out a direction in space. A sample of water in an NMR magnet, left in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings at the temperature TTT, is nearly unpolarised too. Statistical physics then weights the two energy states of a proton in proportion to e−E/kTe^{-E/kT}e−E/kT, where kkk is Boltzmann’s constant. In a field of 1 T1\ \mathrm T1 T at 300 K300\ \mathrm K300 K this gives a Bloch vector of length tanh⁡(ℏγB/2kT)≈3.4⋅10−6\tanh(\hbar\gamma B/2kT)\approx3.4\cdot10^{-6}tanh(ℏγB/2kT)≈3.4⋅10−6. The NMR signal of Lecture 2 comes from turning this small excess into the xyxyxy-plane with a π/2\pi/2π/2-pulse.

3.2 Random preparations

The guess uses only finite probability, which we recall. A random experiment has finitely many outcomes kkk, with probabilities pk>0p_k>0pk​>0 that sum to 111. Outcomes that cannot occur are left out. The conditional probability Prob⁡(b∣k)\Prob(b\mid k)Prob(b∣k) is the probability of an event bbb when kkk is known to have occurred. A reading is an ideal measurement, and a branch of the experiment is one particular sequence of readings.

3.6 What a mixture does not say

The beam from the oven has r=0\blochv=0r=0 and ρ=1211\rho=\tfrac12\Idρ=21​11. The equal mixture of ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ and ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ has this density matrix, and so has the equal mixture of ∣+x⟩\ket{+x}∣+x⟩ and ∣−x⟩\ket{-x}∣−x⟩, or of the two states at ±m\pm\mvec±m for any axis m\mvecm. By Theorem 3.4, no experiment on the atoms tells these preparations apart. This answers the question of §3.1: a density matrix does not record which states were mixed, and nothing measured on the atoms does either.

A mixture is also not a superposition. The state ∣+x⟩=12(∣↑⟩+∣↓⟩)\ket{+x}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket\uparrow+\ket\downarrow)∣+x⟩=2​1​(∣↑⟩+∣↓⟩) and the mixture 1211\tfrac12\Id21​11 of ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ and ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ give the same statistics along zzz. Along xxx, the state ∣+x⟩\ket{+x}∣+x⟩ reads +1+1+1 with certainty, and the mixture with probability 12\tfrac1221​.

In this course every density matrix comes from an ensemble, or, in Lecture 11, from one half of a pair of systems. States of a particle, which need infinitely many dimensions, are not covered. Quantum Mechanics III: Foundations asks what a state is in general, and finds the density matrices again.

The qubit, with mixtures

States: the density matrices ρ=12(11+r⋅σ)\rho=\tfrac12(\Id+\blochv\cdot\sigvec)ρ=21​(11+r⋅σ), the points ∣r∣≤1\abs{\blochv}\le1∣r∣≤1 of the Bloch ball; the pure states are its surface (Theorem 3.6). Observables: as in Lecture 1; the mean of AAA is Tr⁡(ρA)\Tr(\rho A)Tr(ρA). Evolution: ρ↦UρU†\rho\mapsto U\rho\adj Uρ↦UρU†; a Hamiltonian turns the ball rigidly. Measurement: along m\mvecm, the reading ±1\pm1±1 has probability 12(1±m⋅r)\tfrac12(1\pm\mvec\cdot\blochv)21​(1±m⋅r), and the state afterwards is ±m\pm\mvec±m. Summary

  • Statistics depend on the density matrix only: a branch has probability Tr⁡(KρK†)\Tr(K\rho\adj K)Tr(KρK†) and, if this is positive, leaves KρK†K\rho\adj KKρK†, normalised (Theorem 3.4).
  • The Bloch ball: the density matrices of a qubit are the points of the unit ball, and the pure ones its surface (Theorem 3.6).
